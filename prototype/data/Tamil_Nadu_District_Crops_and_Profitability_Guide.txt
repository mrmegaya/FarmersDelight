TAMIL NADU AGRICULTURE GUIDE
District-wise crops and indicative commercial profitability
Prepared: October 2026

PURPOSE AND IMPORTANT NOTES
This guide is a practical overview, not a definitive official crop census. Crop choices vary within each district by block, soil, irrigation, rainfall, season, farm size, labour, access to markets and buyer contracts. District crop lists below identify notable/common crops and suitable examples; they do not mean every crop is grown in every block.

Profitability is not a guaranteed ranking. The indicative tiers reflect potential gross margin and commercial opportunity under suitable conditions, not assured net profit. Prices, yield, input costs and weather can change the order sharply. Calculate local economics before planting, and confirm buyers, water availability, crop duration, transport, grading and storage.

REGIONAL PATTERNS — QUICK SUMMARY
• Cauvery Delta: Thanjavur, Tiruvarur, Nagapattinam, Mayiladuthurai and parts of nearby districts — paddy and suitable rotation crops.
• Western belt: Coimbatore, Erode, Tiruppur, Namakkal, Salem and Karur — turmeric, coconut, maize, tapioca, pulses, cotton, vegetables and fodder, depending on location.
• Mango belt: Krishnagiri and Dharmapuri, plus other suitable areas — mango and associated horticulture.
• Hill regions: Nilgiris and Kodaikanal areas of Dindigul — tea and selected hill vegetables, flowers and fruits.
• Southern dryland/coastal districts: Ramanathapuram, Sivaganga, Virudhunagar, Thoothukudi and parts of Tirunelveli — millets, pulses, cotton, chilli, sesame and other drought-adapted crops.
• Southern irrigated/plantation pockets: Theni, Tenkasi, Kanyakumari and parts of Tirunelveli — banana, paddy, coconut, fruits, spices and vegetables according to local conditions.
• Northern districts: Vellore, Ranipet, Tirupathur, Tiruvannamalai, Villupuram, Kallakurichi, Tiruvallur, Kanchipuram and Chengalpattu — paddy, groundnut, pulses, millets, sugarcane, mango and horticulture according to local conditions.

PART 2 — INDICATIVE COMMERCIAL PROFITABILITY TIERS
These are broad opportunity tiers, not a strict statewide profit ranking. High-value crops can also have high investment, labour, disease, price and market risk.

TIER A — HIGH POTENTIAL, BUT OFTEN HIGHER INVESTMENT OR RISK
1. Flowers: jasmine, marigold, chrysanthemum, tuberose — potentially attractive near reliable wholesale markets, with frequent harvest and labour needs.
2. Vegetables: tomato, chilli, brinjal, gourds, onion and leafy greens — can earn well with good yields and direct market access, but prices can crash and perishability is high.
3. Banana — potentially strong returns with reliable water, suitable variety, good management and confirmed buyers; significant water and input needs.
4. Turmeric — commercially important in suitable western districts; returns depend on yield, curing, storage and market price.
5. Mango — can be attractive in suitable districts and established orchards; income is seasonal and depends on variety, fruit quality, weather and marketing.
6. Grapes and other specialised fruits — potentially profitable in suitable microclimates with skilled management, investment and reliable buyers.
7. Protected cultivation (selected vegetables/flowers) — high potential only where capital, technical skill, quality control and market access are in place.

TIER B — MODERATE TO GOOD POTENTIAL WHEN WELL MATCHED TO LOCAL CONDITIONS
1. Coconut — recurring harvest and multiple product options, but establishment takes time and yield depends on water and management.
2. Groundnut — widely relevant in dryland systems; profitability depends on rainfall/irrigation, yield, seed/input costs and market price.
3. Maize — benefits from established feed and processing demand, but margins depend on input costs and procurement prices.
4. Tapioca — can suit areas with processing facilities and established buyers; transport and factory demand matter.
5. Cashew — suitable areas such as parts of Cuddalore; orchard establishment takes time and price/processing access matter.
6. Cotton — depends on pest management, labour, yield, quality and market prices.
7. Sugarcane — needs substantial water and a viable mill/ buyer arrangement; payment terms and transport costs matter.
8. Fodder crops — can provide dependable local sales where nearby dairy farms or livestock buyers exist.
9. Selected spices (pepper, clove, cardamom) — potentially high-value but location-specific, slow to establish and exposed to weather and disease.

TIER C — OFTEN LOWER PRICE VOLATILITY OR MORE PREDICTABLE MARKETS, BUT NOT ALWAYS HIGHER PROFIT
1. Paddy — established market and procurement pathways in some areas; water, labour, mechanisation and yield determine margins.
2. Millets (ragi, kambu, thinai, samai, varagu) — can fit dryland conditions and niche markets; profitability depends on buyer access, processing and demand.
3. Pulses (black gram, green gram, red gram, horse gram) — can suit rotations and lower-water systems; yields and prices fluctuate.
4. Sesame — potentially suitable for some dryland seasons; harvest and labour management matter.
5. Sorghum — useful in dryland and fodder/feed systems; local demand and yield matter.

SPECIAL CASES
• Medicinal and aromatic crops can look profitable on paper but should generally be planted commercially only after confirming a genuine buyer, accepted species/variety, quality standards and written terms.
• Timber trees may generate large receipts but usually tie up land for years and carry harvest, legal, water and market risks.
• Organic, exotic or export crops do not automatically earn a premium; certification, consistent quality and buyer contracts are important.

PART 3 — HOW TO RANK CROPS FOR A SPECIFIC FARM
Do not compare only the sale price. Estimate net return per acre and return per month.

For each crop, record:
1. Expected saleable yield per acre.
2. Realistic farm-gate price (not retail price).
3. Gross revenue = saleable yield × farm-gate price.
4. Costs: land preparation, seed/planting material, fertiliser, crop protection, water/energy, labour, machinery, harvesting, grading, packing, transport, rent/interest and losses.
5. Net return = gross revenue − total cost.
6. Crop duration and cash-flow timing.
7. Water requirement and local climate fit.
8. Buyer certainty, price volatility, perishability and storage.
9. Labour availability and harvesting frequency.
10. Risk-adjusted return: consider a poor-price or low-yield scenario, not just the best case.

A practical shortlist for a farmer:
• Limited water/dryland: compare locally suitable millets, pulses, sesame and groundnut.
• Reliable irrigation: compare paddy, banana, vegetables, sugarcane and other locally proven crops based on buyer access and costs.
• Near a city or wholesale market: consider vegetables, greens and flowers if labour and daily marketing are manageable.
• Near processing industries: investigate turmeric, tapioca, maize, sugarcane, cotton or cashew only where the relevant buyer/processor is accessible.
• Existing orchard land: compare mango, coconut, banana or other locally proven fruit crops before replanting.

BEFORE INVESTING
Ask the local Agricultural Officer or KVK (Krishi Vigyan Kendra) for current district/block recommendations and recent cost/yield data. Check current market prices through the Tamil Nadu Agricultural Marketing portal and confirm buyers directly. Profitability varies from farm to farm; no crop is guaranteed to be the most profitable.
